Flick
a guide for animators

What you can do with it

You already know how it should move.

Flick is for the moment you can picture a movement — the drawer that slides then thunks, the card that springs in — but you can't turn it into numbers. You show it the motion with your thumb. It gives you the curve and the code.

Start here

If you've drawn a spacing chart, you've drawn an easing curve

You know the little ladder of tick marks animators draw beside a movement — bunched at the ends, spread out in the middle? That's a spacing chart, and it says the same thing an easing curve says: where the thing is at each moment in time.

They're two pictures of one idea. Watch them move together.

Slow out. Starts fast, coasts to a stop. Notice the ticks bunch up at the end — that's the same information as the curve flattening out.

The point

Six things this actually gets you

Observation

Steal a movement from real life

Push a mug across a table. Watch a door swing shut. Then redo that motion with your thumb — Flick writes down the timing you just performed, so you can put it into your animation instead of guessing at it.

Diagnosis

Find out why it feels wrong

"It looks off but I don't know why" is the beginner's whole experience. Flick names it: your motion is front-loaded, or it overshoots 19% and takes 424ms to settle. Once it has a name you can fix it.

Practice

Learn what curve shapes feel like

Flick a snappy motion, look at the curve. Flick a lazy one, look again. After a week of that you can read a curve on sight — you learned it by making them, not by memorising them.

Judgement

Test one timing on five things at once

A timing that's perfect for something sliding can feel sluggish on a fade and wrong on a scale. Flick plays your curve on five different movements together, with the boring default running faintly behind so you can see the difference.

Collection

Keep the ones that work

Save a motion, name it, tag it. Over months you stop reaching for the same default ease and start having your movements — the snap you always use, your favourite settle.

Portability

Take one feeling into every tool

Get the motion once and it comes out as After Effects keyframe influence, or CSS, or code for an app. The same movement, not five slightly different guesses at it.

The words it uses

Five terms, in plain language

Easing
How something speeds up and slows down on its way. Nothing in real life moves at a constant speed, so animation that does looks dead.
Anticipation
The little pull-back before the move — a wind-up. A character crouches before jumping. If you tug your thumb backwards before dragging forward, Flick spots it and measures it.
Overshoot
Going past where you're headed, then coming back. It's what makes motion feel alive and physical rather than mechanical.
Settle
How long the wobble takes to die down after the overshoot. Too fast reads as stiff, too slow reads as floppy.
Bezier vs spring
Two ways of writing a motion down. A bezier is a fixed shape you draw. A spring is a physics rule — how stiff and how bouncy — and it's the honest way to describe something that overshoots and settles. Flick works out which one your gesture actually was.

Your first exercise

The drawer

A real drawer doesn't glide to a stop. It rushes, slows hard, and thunks. Fifteen minutes:

  1. Open a real drawer. Watch only the timing — not the drawer, the speed changes.
  2. Flick that motion three times. Save the one that felt closest, call it Drawer.
  3. Look at what Flick says about it. Was it front-loaded? Did you overshoot?
  4. Now flick a deliberately wrong version — smooth and even. Compare the two curves.

The gap between those two curves is the thing you're learning to see.
